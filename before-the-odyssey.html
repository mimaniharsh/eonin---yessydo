Before he could go home, there was a war.

Nolan's Odyssey starts after Troy has already burned. This is the ten years that came first: how it began, which gods picked sides, and what actually happened with Helen.

None of this spoils the film. It's the part the film assumes you know.

About eight minutes. Read this one first, then the Odyssey guide.

Where all of this happens

Real places. You can fly to every one of them.

On a phone, swipe the map sideways.

  • TroyA rich walled city on the coast of what's now Turkey, guarding the sea route to the Black Sea. Ruled by old King Priam, defended by his son Hector. It was a real place: a German businessman dug it up in the 1870s.
  • SpartaHelen's home, and her husband Menelaus's kingdom. Where the trouble starts.
  • MycenaeThe richest, bossiest kingdom in Greece, ruled by Agamemnon, Menelaus's older brother. He ends up in charge of the whole war.
  • IthacaA small rocky island on the far side of Greece. Odysseus's home. About as far from Troy as a Greek could be.
  • AulisThe beach where a thousand ships gathered before sailing east.
  • OlympusGreece's tallest mountain. The gods live on top and watch everything below like it's television.

The gods, in one page

They're a family. An immortal, gorgeous, deeply petty family, and they treat human wars as a hobby.

Zeus is the father and king. His siblings and children run everything else: the sea, war, love, wisdom, the sun. They can't die, they can change shape, and they fall in love with humans constantly, which is why half the heroes in this story have a god for a parent. They aren't good or evil. They have favourites, and they hold grudges, and the Trojan War is basically what happens when they can't agree.

Rooting for the Greeks

  • HeraQueen of the gods, Zeus's wife. Furious at Troy because a Trojan prince said she wasn't the prettiest. Never forgives.
  • AthenaGoddess of wisdom and clever warfare. Same grudge as Hera. Odysseus is her favourite human because he thinks the way she does.
  • PoseidonGod of the sea. Once built Troy's walls and got stiffed on payment, so he's happy to see them fall.
  • ThetisA sea nymph, and Achilles's mother. Not powerful, but Zeus owes her a favour, and she calls it in for her son.

Rooting for Troy

  • AphroditeGoddess of love. Won the beauty contest that started it all, so she owes Troy. Personally protects Paris, which he needs.
  • ApolloGod of the sun, archery, plague and prophecy. Troy's great protector. His arrows kill both Patroclus and Achilles.
  • AresGod of war, the ugly kind. Loud, brave, not very bright. Athena beats him up at one point.
  • ZeusOfficially neutral. Actually fond of Troy, but he lets fate play out and keeps the others from wrecking it entirely.

Two more you'll meet: Hermes, the messenger, who runs errands for Zeus; and Eris, goddess of discord, who has one job in this story and does it perfectly.

How it started: a wedding, an apple, a bribe

The gods throw a wedding for Thetis, the sea nymph, and a mortal king called Peleus. Everyone is invited except Eris, goddess of discord, for obvious reasons. She shows up anyway, tosses a golden apple onto the table with the words for the fairest, and leaves.

Three goddesses grab for it: Hera, Athena, Aphrodite. Zeus is too smart to judge his own wife against his own daughters, so he sends them to a human. A handsome shepherd on a hill near Troy named Paris, who happens to be a lost prince of the city.

Each goddess offers a bribe. Hera: power over all of Asia. Athena: wisdom and glory in war. Aphrodite: the most beautiful woman in the world as your wife. Paris picks Aphrodite. He is about nineteen.

The small print: the most beautiful woman in the world is already married.

Helen

The face that launched a thousand ships. Literally: the Greek fleet was a thousand-odd boats.

Helen is the daughter of Zeus, who visited her mother, Leda, in the form of a swan. (This is a real thing that happens in Greek myth. Don't think about it too hard.) She grows up in Sparta so beautiful that every king in Greece turns up to court her, including a young Odysseus. Her father is terrified: whoever he picks, the losers will start a war.

Odysseus, already the clever one, suggests a fix. Make every suitor swear an oath first: whoever wins Helen, all the rest will defend that marriage if anyone ever takes her. They swear. Helen goes to Menelaus, king of Sparta. Everyone goes home. Problem solved, for a few years.

Then Paris arrives in Sparta on a friendly visit. Menelaus, being a good host, goes off to a funeral in Crete and leaves his guest alone with his wife for a week. When he comes back, Helen, Paris, and a fair amount of the treasury are gone.

Did she go willingly? Homer never quite says. Some versions have Paris kidnap her. Some have her fall for him, with Aphrodite's thumb on the scale. In the Iliad she calls herself a shameless dog and wishes she'd died before it started, which sounds like guilt rather than a hostage. Later poets couldn't stand it and invented a version where the real Helen sat out the whole war in Egypt and Troy got a ghost. Three thousand years on, people are still arguing. The film has Lupita Nyong'o playing her, so watch what she does with it.

Menelaus calls in the oath. Every king who swore it has to come. Which is how a wedding present becomes the biggest army the world had seen.

Getting the band together

Not everyone wants to go. Odysseus has a new wife, Penelope, and a baby son. When the recruiters land on Ithaca he pretends to have gone mad, ploughing the beach and sowing it with salt. One of them puts baby Telemachus down in front of the plough. Odysseus swerves. Caught. He's the man who invented the oath and now it's dragged him in too.

Achilles, the greatest fighter alive, has been told by his mother that if he goes to Troy he'll die young and famous, and if he stays home he'll die old and forgotten. She hides him on an island disguised as a girl. Odysseus turns up as a merchant with a basket of jewellery and one sword in it. Only one "girl" reaches for the sword. Caught.

The fleet gathers at Aulis and can't leave: no wind for weeks. A priest tells Agamemnon the goddess Artemis is offended and wants his daughter, Iphigenia, as a sacrifice. He sends for her, telling her mother it's for a wedding. Then he kills her. The wind comes. Remember this. His wife, Clytemnestra, does.

The war, in eight beats

Ten years of siege. Homer's Iliad only covers a few weeks near the end, but they're the weeks that matter.

  1. Nine years of nothing

    Troy's walls are too strong. The Greeks camp on the beach, raid the countryside, and get older. Achilles does most of the winning. Hector, Priam's eldest, does most of the defending. Paris mostly stays inside.

  2. Two men fight over a girl

    Agamemnon has to give back a captured woman to appease Apollo, so he takes Achilles's captured woman, Briseis, instead. Achilles, humiliated, refuses to fight. This is the first line of the Iliad: Sing, goddess, the rage of Achilles. The whole poem is about one man sulking, and what it costs.

  3. Without him, the Greeks lose

    Zeus, doing Thetis a favour, lets the Trojans push the Greeks back to their ships. Hector starts setting them on fire. Odysseus and the other kings go to Achilles's tent and beg. He says no.

  4. Patroclus

    Achilles's closest friend (and, most readers think, more than that) can't stand watching. He borrows Achilles's armour, drives the Trojans back, goes too far, and Hector kills him. Achilles's grief is the most terrifying thing in the poem.

  5. Achilles and Hector

    Achilles gets new armour from the gods and goes back out for one reason. He chases Hector three times round the walls of Troy, kills him, ties the body to his chariot and drags it through the dust for days while Hector's parents watch from the wall.

  6. An old man in the tent

    King Priam sneaks into the Greek camp alone at night, kneels, and kisses the hands that killed his son, asking for the body back. Achilles thinks of his own father and weeps. He gives Hector back. That's where the Iliad ends: not with Troy falling, but with two enemies crying together.

  7. Achilles dies

    Not long after. Paris, of all people, shoots him, with Apollo steering the arrow into the one spot on his heel his mother missed when she dipped him in the river of the dead as a baby. Paris dies soon after that too. Nobody misses him.

  8. The horse

    Odysseus's idea. The Greeks build a giant wooden horse, hide their best men inside, burn their camp and sail away out of sight. A Greek "deserter" tells the Trojans it's a gift to the gods. A priest named Laocoön says don't trust it; two sea serpents eat him. Cassandra, Priam's daughter, screams that the city is about to fall; she's cursed to be right and never believed. The Trojans wheel it in and throw a party. That night the Greeks climb out, open the gates, and Troy burns.

Priam is killed at his own altar. Hector's baby son is thrown from the walls. Helen is found in the palace; Menelaus raises his sword to kill her, she opens her robe, and he drops it. He takes her home. The Greeks load the ships with loot and prisoners and set off for Greece, having offended just about every god on the way out.

That's the moment Nolan's film begins. Odysseus, the man whose idea won the war, has a short sail home to a wife he hasn't seen in ten years. What happens next is in the other guide, and in the cinema.

The names you'll hear

  • Agamemnon Mycenae
    Commander of the Greeks. Proud, greedy, not very likeable. Sacrificed his own daughter to get the wind.
  • Menelaus Sparta
    His younger brother. Helen's husband. Decent, a bit dull, genuinely heartbroken.
  • Achilles Phthia, northern Greece
    Half god, fastest and deadliest man alive, and knows he'll die here. Chose fame over a long life.
  • Odysseus Ithaca
    Not the strongest, the smartest. Every trick in this story is his.
  • Hector Troy
    Priam's eldest, Troy's best fighter, a husband and father who never wanted the war. The most decent man in the poem, and he's on the losing side.
  • Paris Troy
    Hector's little brother. Pretty, cowardly, good with a bow. Started everything.
  • Priam Troy
    The old king. Has fifty sons and watches most of them die.
  • Clytemnestra Mycenae
    Agamemnon's wife, Helen's sister. Spends ten years planning what she'll do when he comes home. (Also Lupita Nyong'o, in the film. That's not an accident.)

Who wrote it all down

  • Homer, around 700 BC. Possibly one blind poet, possibly a name for generations of singers who performed these stories from memory before anyone wrote them down. The Iliad is the war; the Odyssey is the trip home. Both are still the best versions.
  • Virgil, a Roman, wrote the Aeneid around 20 BC from the losing side: a Trojan prince escapes the burning city carrying his father on his back and goes on to found Rome. It's where the fullest account of the wooden horse comes from, and the line "beware Greeks bearing gifts".
  • The Athenian playwrights, 400s BC. Aeschylus wrote what Clytemnestra does when Agamemnon gets home. Euripides wrote The Trojan Women, about what happens to Helen, Cassandra and Hector's widow after the city falls. Bleak, brilliant, still staged.
  • Christopher Marlowe, 1590s, gave Helen her most famous line: "Was this the face that launched a thousand ships?"
  • Now. Madeline Miller's The Song of Achilles (the Achilles and Patroclus love story; bring tissues). Pat Barker's The Silence of the Girls, the war from Briseis's point of view. Emily Wilson's translations of both poems, which Nolan reportedly used.
